# Episode 4, "Do heavy things fall faster?": script and shot plan (Gate 1)

The one sentence: **no. Heavy and light things fall equally fast: the planet pulls harder on the heavy one, but the
heavy one is exactly that much harder to get moving. Only air makes a difference.**

Planned length about 89 s (80 to 90). Speech sets the timing, so the report times will come from the takes.

## The script

**Cold open:** Peek (take `drop`): "Heavy... light... for SCIENCE!" Then Peek's existing "Oh... no." (`audio/voice/p1-ohno`, the terrified whisper from Episode 1; no new take).

**Report A (Peek, normal report voice, slightly hushed, 71 words):**
> Commander. Field report number four.
> I dropped a bowling ball and an apple. Same height, same moment. They landed together. Right next to an agent.
> Remember report one: the heavier the thing, the harder the pull. The ball is forty times heavier than the apple, so
> their planet pulls on it forty times harder.
> Theory: forty times the pull, forty times faster.
> Wrong. They land together. Every time. Because the ball...

**Interruption, Crowe (take `crowe`, first two lines):** "Attention, shoppers." / "Have you seen... this?"

**Report B (Peek, low, hushed, urgent, fully voiced, the Episode 2/3 Report B direction, 106 words):**
> ...is also forty times harder to get moving.
> Forty times the pull, for forty times the stuff. It evens out. Everything speeds up the same: twenty-two miles an
> hour faster, every second.
> Except a feather. Air pushes back on everything, but on light, wide things, it wins.
> On their Moon there is almost no air. An astronaut dropped a hammer and a feather there. They landed together.
> So: heavier means more pull, but also harder to move. Only air gets in the way.
> Which raises a question, Commander. Up there, astronauts bounce like bunnies. So why do you weigh less on the Moon?
> Investigating. Peek out.

**Tag:** Crowe's detector plays Peek's own "...Peek out." back, small and tinny. This reuses the end of the Report B take through a radio filter, at no cost. Then Crowe (take `crowe`, last line): "...Peek."

Report total: 177 words (Episode 3 was 170, at 89 s). If the takes run long, I shorten the silent beats first and never speed up speech.

**How it sits beside Episode 1.** Report A quotes Episode 1 ("the heavier the thing, the harder the pull"), says it is still true, and builds the wrong theory on it. Report B shows why the harder pull does not mean a faster fall: forty times the pull acts on forty times the stuff. It then reuses Episode 1's own number, 22 mph faster every second, and the new point is that this holds for everything. Wording I checked against the brief:
- Never "gravity pulls on everything equally". The planet pulls on the ball forty times harder; what is equal is how fast they speed up.
- Never "wants to fall".
- The wrong guess is labelled PEEK'S THEORY.

## Tag (about 6 s)

The service desk, medium, Crowe fully in frame with the plush. His wrist detector crackles, and Peek's voice comes out
small and tinny: "...Peek out." (We are on Crowe and the glowing detector. **Rule 3 needs your call:** the voice belongs to
Peek, who is not in frame; the device that plays it is.) Crowe slowly lifts the plush to his face: "...Peek." Now he has a
name. NEXT REPORT / WHY DO YOU WEIGH LESS ON THE MOON? lands over a dark band. Cut to black.

## Facts (two spoken numbers: forty times, 22 mph)

| Fact | Value used | Source / check |
|---|---|---|
| Heaviest bowling ball | 16 lb (7.26 kg), the USBC maximum | USBC equipment specifications (16.00 lb maximum, no minimum), e.g. bowlingball.com BowlVersity |
| A medium apple | 182 g (0.40 lb) | USDA SNAP-Ed: 1 medium apple, 182 g |
| "Forty times heavier" | 7.26 / 0.182 = 39.9; said "forty times"; screen ×40 (no pounds spoken, so no kg needed) | computed |
| "Forty times harder" pull | the pull between the planet and a thing is proportional to the thing's mass, so ×40 | Newton's law of gravitation |
| "Forty times harder to get moving" | for the same push, the speeding-up is inversely proportional to mass | Newton's second law |
| Speeding up | g = 9.81 m/s² = 21.9 mph per second = 35.3 km/h per second; said "twenty-two miles an hour faster, every second" (as in Episode 1); screen 35 km/h per second | NIST standard gravity 9.80665 m/s²; computed |
| Cold-open drop | 4.9 m takes 1.0 s; air makes a difference of under 0.01 s between ball and apple (apple terminal speed about 37 m/s) | computed |
| Air on a feather | the drag is about the same size as its weight within a fraction of a second, so it drifts | standard physics |
| Moon air | the Moon has only an extremely thin exosphere; said "almost no air" | NASA (Moon facts; NSSDC hammer-feather page: "essentially in a vacuum") |
| Hammer and feather | Apollo 15, Commander David Scott, 2 Aug 1971, at the end of the last moonwalk. He dropped a 1.32 kg aluminium geologic hammer and a falcon feather from about 1.6 m at the same moment; they reached the ground together. | NASA NSSDC "The Apollo 15 Hammer-Feather Drop" (nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/lunar/apollo_15_feather_drop.html); NASA Science "The Apollo 15 Hammer-Feather Drop" |
| What I do not say | The feather's mass (NASA's own page says the 0.03 kg in the 1971 report is probably a typo), the year, and the astronaut's name. On screen: APOLLO 15 only. | NSSDC page |
| Astronauts bounce like bunnies | Apollo moonwalkers often moved in a two-footed "bunny hop" | NASA Apollo surface footage and lunar surface journal descriptions |
