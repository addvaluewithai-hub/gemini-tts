# Earth Report: The Network 1

Earth Report field file: The Network, part 1 of 3, English version. Adapted from `../explain-network-01-ar`, not translated: same facts, order and boards; lines and jokes rewritten for English speakers ('Can you hear me now?' in place of the Arabic 'hello, hello'; the first text message's 160 characters give the planet LOL; the SIM tray pin nobody can find replaces 'they lose SIMs more than house keys'; Cooper's call is a humblebrag). How a voice gets from one mobile phone to another: sound becomes numbers, the phone is a radio that only reaches the nearest tower, the rest is cable, why the network is called cellular, how it knows where each phone is, the SIM and roaming, why calls drop, the history from the first handheld call to 5G, and phones that talk to satellites.

Facts are stated from web research on 12 November 2026 (see explain-network-01-ar/plan.md for links) and from general knowledge of how mobile networks work: 8.8 billion mobile connections and 5.8 billion unique subscribers (GSMA, March 2026) against a world population of about 8.2 billion; narrowband telephone speech is sampled 8,000 times a second; Martin Cooper of Motorola made the first handheld mobile call on 3 April 1973 in New York to Joel Engel of Bell Labs; the prototype weighed about 1.1 kg, took about ten hours to charge for about thirty minutes of talk, and the commercial model sold in 1983 for about 4,000 dollars; NTT opened the first commercial cellular network in Tokyo in 1979; the first GSM call was made in Finland on 1 July 1991; the first text message was sent on 3 December 1992 and read Merry Christmas; 3G launched commercially in 2001, 4G in 2009 and 5G in 2019; 5G covered 55 percent of the world's population in 2025, 84 percent in high-income and 4 percent in low-income countries (ITU); direct-to-phone satellite texting has been sold commercially since July 2025 and voice was still in testing in 2026; emergency calls work without a SIM in many countries, not all; a text message was limited to 160 characters because it travelled in spare room in the signalling channel; before cellular, car telephone systems served a whole city from one mast with a handful of channels.

## Script

### intro

Sir. A new field file from planet Earth. The network file. Report one: the mobile phone. The device every human carries in a pocket. This planet has a little over eight billion humans... and almost nine billion mobile lines. More lines than humans. The owners of those lines are only about six billion humans. So plenty of them carry two or three lines. And answer none of them. I will report how this device carries a voice from one pocket to another, why it is called cellular, and why the call drops in the elevator. And the strangest thing I found: almost none of them know where their voice actually goes. They talk into it every day... and never wonder.

### 1. SOUND BECOMES NUMBERS

Start at the beginning of the trip. A human talks, so the air shakes. And the microphone turns that shaking into a wave of electricity. The phone measures that wave thousands of times a second. On an ordinary call: eight thousand measurements every second. Why eight thousand? Because human speech does not need more. Music needs a lot more. Which is why hold music on the phone sounds that terrible. Each measurement becomes a number. From here on, the wave itself no longer matters. What travels is the numbers. Then the phone compresses them: it throws away everything a human ear will not notice. So the voice that arrives, sir, is not your voice. It is a summary of your voice. And those numbers leave the phone... through the air.

### 2. YOUR PHONE IS A RADIO

Through the air how? Here is the first surprise. A mobile phone is not a telephone. A mobile phone is a radio. Humans can see light with their eyes. Radio waves are exactly the same kind of thing, moving at the same speed. Just longer... and their eyes cannot see them. So every one of them walks around with a small radio station in a pocket, sending and receiving at the same time. And not just one station. The phone talks to the tower. Wi-Fi is a second radio. The earbuds in their ears: a third. And the map that knows where you are is listening to satellites. That is four. And the airplane mode button does exactly one thing: it silences all of those stations. One human gives off four signals just standing at a bus stop. And the whole planet is soaking in waves they cannot see. I can see them, sir. It is a very busy view.

### 3. THE TRIP OF A CALL

And the second surprise: that wave does not travel far. Only to the nearest tower. A few hundred meters, or a few kilometers. From the tower, the voice goes down into a cable under the ground, to the heart of the network: a building full of computers. The network core finds the other phone, and sends the voice down a cable to the tower nearest to her. That core does three things: it knows who you are, it knows where the other person is, and it bills you. And the third one is the only one that never goes down. And only the last few hundred meters... are radio again. So wireless, sir, is only wireless at the very start and the very end. Everything in between... is wire. They called it wireless. It is the most wired thing on the planet. And all of this takes less than a quarter of a second. He talks, and she hears him while he is still finishing the sentence.

### 4. WHY CELLULAR?

So why do they call it cellular? Because the land is divided into cells. And every cell has a tower. The trick: there are only so many channels in the air. So each cell gets one set, and a cell that does not touch it reuses that same set. The same air... sold a thousand times over. In cities the cells are small, a tower every few blocks, because of the crowds. In the desert, a single cell can stretch for dozens of kilometers. Each tower carries several antennas, each facing a different way. And the phone's own signal is very weak, weaker than a small light bulb. The tower is the one with the big ears. When the signal gets weak, the phone raises its voice so the tower can hear it. That is why the battery drains faster where the reception is bad. And many of their towns do not like how towers look. So they disguise them as palm trees, or pine trees. A poor disguise, sir. To be fair, it is better than mine. And here is the most important fact for any spy: a phone sitting quietly in a pocket keeps whispering to the nearest tower: I am here. It has to. Otherwise, how would the network deliver a call to it? And from the strength of that signal at several towers around it, the network can work out roughly where it is. Without any satellites at all. So the network knows where every single one of them is. All the time. The humans pay a monthly subscription for a tracking device... and get upset when the data runs out. And when someone is driving, the call is handed from one tower to the next. In a fraction of a second. They never notice. And when the handover fails? Can you hear me now? The most repeated sentence in the history of the planet.

### 5. THE SIM, AND ROAMING

And how does the network know who you are? From the SIM. A sliver of plastic the size of a fingernail. That is your identity. Not the phone. Move it to another phone... and your number moves with it. It holds a secret key the network uses to check that it is not a copy. And the newest ones are not even plastic anymore. They are built into the phone itself. The phone also has its own separate number. So if it is stolen, the network can lock it in the thief's face. And the phone number itself is an address. The first digits say: which country. The next ones: which carrier. And the rest: you. So the network reads a number the way a mail carrier reads an envelope. When you travel to another country, your phone finds a foreign network and introduces itself. The foreign network asks your home network: is this one yours? And your network answers: yes. And he will pay. He will pay a lot. Every network on the planet talks to every other one and settles the bill. Some of their countries do not speak to each other at all... and their phone networks get along fine. Money unites, sir.

### 6. WHY DO CALLS DROP?

So why does the call drop? Reason one: distance. The further from the tower, the weaker the wave. Reason two: walls. Concrete and steel swallow the wave. Reason three: the elevator. A closed metal box. The best radio shield humans ever invented... and they step into it mid-call. And reason four: crowds. Every tower can only carry so many. At a game or a concert, everyone is holding up a phone... and the tower says: enough. And the bars at the top of the screen? They measure signal strength, not how crowded the tower is. You can have five bars... and nothing loads. The bars are there to calm them down. In fairness: the emergency number works in many of their countries even without a SIM, on any network that is there. The first time I have seen them agree on something useful.

### 7. THE STORY

Before cells, there were car phones. One tall mast for the entire city. With just a handful of channels. Meaning a handful of calls at the same time... for a whole city. Anyone who wanted to talk waited in line. So it was the idea of cells that made the mobile phone. Not the device. The story starts on April 3rd, 1973. An engineer named Martin Cooper stood on a sidewalk in New York... and made the first call in history from a handheld mobile phone. And who did he call? His rival, at the other company that was trying to build the same device. To tell him: I am calling you from the street. The first mobile call in the history of the planet... was a humblebrag. I respect this creature. The device weighed more than a kilogram. It took ten hours to charge... for half an hour of talk. And when it went on sale ten years later, it cost about four thousand dollars. The first commercial network opened in Tokyo in 1979. The first generation: voice only. And anyone with the right radio could listen to your call. The second generation, in 1991: the voice turned into numbers, and text messages came with it. The first text was sent in December 1992. It said: Merry Christmas. And a text was only a hundred and sixty characters. Because it squeezed into spare room inside the network's own signals. The humans invented an entire language of abbreviations to fit into a spare slot. L. O. L. The third generation brought the internet into the phone. The fourth turned everything into internet. Even the voice itself. And the fifth, from 2019: much faster. And not only for humans. For machines talking to each other. With every generation, the humans say: this is the one that will change our lives. What actually changes: they watch the same videos... in higher resolution. Today, 5G covers more than half the planet's population. But not fairly. In rich countries: eighty-four percent. In poor ones: four.

### 8. THE TOWERS WENT TO SPACE

And the latest development I recorded: the towers have gone to space. Since 2025, an ordinary phone can send texts straight to a satellite. No dish, no special equipment. In the desert, at sea, anywhere that can see the sky. That satellite is hundreds of kilometers up, moving at about twenty-seven thousand kilometers an hour. And the phone in a pocket talks to it. My own ship cannot do that, sir. Calls are still being tested. But the direction is clear: no place on this planet will stay out of coverage. Which, sir, is bad news for us. I used to hide in those places.

### 9. THE HUMAN DICTIONARY

Two words from the Human Dictionary. Cell: the patch of land one tower covers. That is where the cell phone got its name. And SIM: the little card that carries your identity. And the tiny pin you need to open its tray? No human has ever found one when they needed it.

### 10. SUMMARY

Summary of the report, sir. One: the phone is a radio, and it only talks to the nearest tower. Two: everything after that is cable. Three: the network has to know where you are... to reach you. Which leaves a question: where do those cables go? And this internet they keep talking about... where exactly does it live? Next report: the internet. And the answer, sir... is at the bottom of the sea. This has been Earth Report. Agent Peek. End of transmission.
