# Earth Report: The Network 2

Earth Report field file: The Network, part 2 of 3, English version. Adapted from `../explain-network-02-ar`, not translated: same facts, order and boards; lines and jokes rewritten for English speakers (the people passing a bag down a line becomes a bucket brigade; new lines: the senator's 'series of tubes', the 'someone else's computer' sticker, and a company locking its keys inside). Where the internet physically is: a network of networks with nobody in charge, the cables on the sea floor that carry almost everything between continents, how they break and are mended, packets and why the network survives a cut, names and numbers, two times the routing map went wrong, what the cloud is, Wi-Fi, who uses the cables now, satellites, and who is still offline. The 'series of tubes' remark is US Senator Ted Stevens, 2006 (general knowledge).

Facts are stated from web research on 12 November 2026 (see explain-network-01-ar/plan.md for links) and from general knowledge of how the internet works: the first ARPANET message on 29 October 1969 was LO because the receiving machine crashed during LOGIN; about 607 submarine cable systems were in service in 2026 with more than 1.5 million km of cable, carrying well over 95 percent of intercontinental traffic (TeleGeography); deep-sea cable is about as thick as a garden hose and repeaters sit roughly every 80 km; light in fibre travels at about 200,000 km a second; the 1858 transatlantic telegraph cable carried Queen Victoria's 98-word message in about 16 hours and failed after about three weeks, after excessive voltage was applied; there are 150 to 200 cable faults a year, 70 to 80 percent from fishing and anchors, and fish bites cause under 1 percent (ICPC); cable repair ships number in the low tens worldwide; Red Sea cables were cut in February 2024 and September 2025; on 24 February 2008 Pakistan Telecom's routing announcement took YouTube offline worldwide for about two hours; on 4 October 2021 a faulty configuration change withdrew Facebook's routes for about six hours and staff badges stopped working (New York Times); the name Wi-Fi was coined in 1999 by a branding firm and stands for nothing; Google, Meta, Amazon and Microsoft use about three quarters of international cable capacity (estimates citing TeleGeography) and Meta announced a 50,000 km cable in February 2025; one satellite network had more than 11,000 satellites in orbit in November 2026; 6 billion people were online and 2.2 billion offline in 2025 (ITU); the web was proposed in 1989; a modern cable costs hundreds of millions of dollars and carries hundreds of terabits a second; cable ships lay at walking pace and bury the cable near shore; intelligence agencies have tapped submarine cables (reported since 2013); IPv4 has about 4.3 billion addresses and roughly half of traffic to large sites now uses IPv6; data centres used about 1.5 percent of world electricity in 2024 (IEA); the 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi band is shared with microwave ovens.

## Script

### intro

Sir. The second report in the network file. Today's subject: the internet. Where exactly is it? And who owns it? In the last report we learned the phone only talks to the nearest tower, and everything after that is cable. Today I follow those cables to the end. When you ask humans where their photos and files are, they point up and say: in the cloud. There is no cloud, sir. I looked. What I found: hoses on the ocean floor... and warehouses. I will report what those hoses are, who cuts them, and how a message finds its way from one continent to another. And how one of the biggest companies on the planet erased itself from the map for six hours... and its own staff could not get into the building.

### 1. A NETWORK OF NETWORKS

First: the internet is not one network. In your home there is a small network: a few devices. That one connects to your phone company's network. And that connects to bigger networks that join countries and continents. Tens of thousands of networks, each with its own owner, and they agree on exactly one thing: how to talk. No manager. No head office. And no button that switches it all off. And by the way: the internet is not the websites. The websites were built on top of it twenty years later. The internet is the plumbing. The websites are the water. It started in October 1969. Two computers at two universities. They wanted to send one word: LOGIN. Two letters arrived. L, O. And the machine crashed. The first message in the history of the internet was a crash. And the humans have carried on in that spirit ever since.

### 2. HOSES ON THE SEA FLOOR

So how are the continents connected? The humans assume: satellites. Wrong. Traffic between continents runs through cables. Lying on the bottom of the ocean. More than ninety-five percent of it. Their photos, their money, their calls, their governments' secrets... all on the sea floor. More than six hundred cables in service. Together they run about one and a half million kilometers. Enough to wrap around the planet more than thirty-five times. And they carry almost everything that crosses from one continent to another. And out in the deep ocean, the cable itself is about as thick as a garden hose. Inside are strands of glass, each one thinner than a human hair. And what travels through them is not electricity. It is light. Laser pulses, switching on and off billions of times a second. A modern cable costs hundreds of millions of dollars, and every second it carries enough for millions of movies playing at once. So the entire civilization of these creatures runs on a few threads of glass inside a hose... lying in the dark, kilometers down. And nobody is guarding it. One of their senators once called the internet a series of tubes. He was laughed at for years. For the record, sir: he was not entirely wrong. Light in glass travels at about two hundred thousand kilometers a second. So from one continent to another in less than a tenth of a second. And every eighty kilometers or so there is a booster for the light, on the sea floor, powered by the cable itself. Who lays them? A ship moving at walking pace, with the cable sinking behind it to the bottom. For months. And near the shore, they bury it in the sand. And it comes out of the sea into a small building on the beach that looks like an ordinary storage shed. No sign. I walked past one, sir. Nobody asked me where I was going.

### 3. SINCE 1858

And the idea is very old. The first cable under the ocean was laid in 1858. For the telegraph. A queen sent a message of congratulations down it to a president. Ninety-eight words. It took about sixteen hours. And the cable died three weeks later. Because one of them turned up the voltage to make it go faster. Sixteen hours was a miracle back then. Before that, a message took ten days by ship. Today, humans close the page if it takes three seconds.

### 4. THEY BREAK

And these hoses break. A hundred and fifty to two hundred faults every year. Most are not sabotage. A fishing net dragged along the bottom. Or a ship dropping anchor in the wrong place. And the number of ships on the entire planet that know how to repair these cables... is very small. A few dozen. The ship goes to the spot, drops a hook, pulls both ends up from the bottom, and splices them on deck. Strand by strand. It can take weeks. And because everything runs through these hoses, their intelligence agencies have been tapping them for years. So I am not the first spy on these cables, sir. Just the first one from out of town. There is a famous suspect: the shark. It really does bite cables. It has been filmed doing it. But its share of the faults is under one percent. The real culprit is always... a human, fishing. And when a cable is cut? In the Red Sea, cables were cut in 2024, and again in 2025. The internet slowed down across Asia and the Middle East. But it did not go down. Why not? Here is the cleverest thing in the whole invention.

### 5. PACKETS

Anything sent over the network, a photo, a message, a movie... is cut into small pieces. They call them packets. Each packet carries its own number, and the address it is going to. And each packet travels on its own. By whatever road is free. One goes this way, another goes by a different continent entirely. Every router along the way looks at the address and pushes the packet on to the next one. Like a bucket brigade. And none of them sees the whole journey. At the far end, they are put back in order. And if one went missing... it gets requested again. That is why, when a cable is cut, the packets just go around another way. Without anyone telling them to. This network was designed from the start to keep going while broken. The cat photo one human sends to another: half of it may pass under one ocean, and the other half under a different ocean. And a whole cat arrives. I do not understand their priorities... but the engineering is respectable.

### 6. NAMES AND THE MAP

Every device on the network has a number. That is its address. But humans do not memorize numbers. So they built a directory. You type the website's name. The directory answers with the number. And with that number, your packets know where to go. All of this before the page even opens. In a fraction of a second. And those numbers ran out. The original system had about four billion of them, and the devices now far outnumber that. So they made a new system with numbers that never run out. And after many, many years... only about half the network has moved over. So who tells the packets which road to take? Every network announces to the others: these addresses are with me. And the others... believe it. Yes, sir. The entire map of the internet runs on trust. In 2008, the government of Pakistan ordered YouTube blocked inside the country. And a phone company there accidentally announced to the entire world: YouTube is over here, with me. And the world believed it. YouTube vanished from the planet for about two hours. And in 2021, Facebook made a bad configuration change... from inside the company. Which erased its addresses from the map. Facebook and all of its apps. Six hours. Three billion humans unable to message each other. The engineers could not fix it remotely, because the network was down. And when they went to the building... their door badges would not work. Because they ran on the same network. Neither time was an attack. Both times, it was an employee... pressing the wrong button. A company that locked itself out, and left the keys inside. Sir, I have been sending Command reports on their weapons. I should be sending reports on their mistakes. They are more dangerous.

### 7. THE CLOUD

Back to the cloud. Where are all these packets going? To buildings the size of football stadiums. Inside are rows of computers, running day and night. And air conditioning that never stops. That is the cloud. As one of their own stickers puts it: there is no cloud. It is just someone else's computer. They named it a cloud so nobody would think about it. These warehouses use about one and a half percent of all the electricity on the planet. And the number is climbing fast... because of artificial intelligence. The trip of a single page starts at your device. The first few meters: radio. The Wi-Fi. The router takes it and puts it on a cable. The phone company drops it to the bottom of the sea. And it reaches the warehouse, on another continent. And the answer comes back the same way. All while your finger is still lifting off the screen. And Wi-Fi is a radio, just like the mobile phone. Only for the last few meters. And its most common frequency is the same one used by the microwave ovens they heat their food in. Which is why the internet sometimes slows down... while they reheat dinner. And the name? Invented in 1999. A branding company came up with it. And it means nothing. Humans ask for the Wi-Fi password everywhere they go. Not one of them has asked what the name means.

### 8. WHO OWNS IT?

So who owns the hoses? It used to be the phone companies. Today, four tech companies use about three quarters of the capacity between continents. And they lay cables under their own names now. One of them announced a cable fifty thousand kilometers long. Longer than the planet is around. We said nobody owns the internet. But somebody is holding the pipes. And their governments now argue over who lays the cable, where it runs, and who repairs it. The garden hose has become politics. And satellites? They exist. One network alone has more than eleven thousand of them. And they help in places the cables do not reach: mountains, deserts, ships. But the real load is still on the sea floor. And one last number, sir: six billion humans are online. And two point two billion are still outside. More than a quarter of the planet. Most of them in poor countries.

### 9. THE HUMAN DICTIONARY

Two words from the Human Dictionary. IP: a device's number on the network. The address its packets go to. And cloud: someone else's computer, in a warehouse, in another country. A fancy name for something boring.

### 10. SUMMARY

Summary of the report, sir. One: the internet is a network of networks, and nobody is in charge. Two: the continents are joined by hoses of glass on the sea floor. Three: everything is cut into packets, and every packet finds its way. Which leaves the strangest use of all: a human talking to another human on a different continent... and seeing their face. At the same moment. The next report, and the last in this file: the video call. And how your face is cut into a thousand pieces and put back together. This has been Earth Report. Agent Peek. End of transmission.
